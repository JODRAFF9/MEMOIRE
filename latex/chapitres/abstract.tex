\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

This thesis evaluates the impact of migrant
remittances on child multidimensional poverty in Senegal, where more
than half of all children are deprived in at least four dimensions of
their well-being and nearly a quarter of households receive transfers
from abroad. It draws on the two waves of the Harmonized Survey on
Household Living Conditions (EHCVM), 2018-2019 and 2021-2022, which allow
17,772 children to be followed from one wave to the other. Poverty is
measured at the child level using the Multiple Overlapping Deprivation
Analysis (MODA) of the United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF), with seven
dimensions and thirteen indicators tailored to three age groups, and
the outcome variable is the number of dimensions in deprivation.
Children in households receiving in both waves are compared with
children in households that never receive, through propensity score
matching conducted separately within each age group, the score
including each child's initial deprivation level; the robustness of the
result is tested against the treatment definition, the deprivation
cut-off, the three matching algorithms and Rosenbaum's sensitivity
test. The descriptive analysis sets the starting point, the incidence
of multidimensional poverty falling from 60.4 to 51.9 percent between
the two waves and children in recipient households starting from a much
lower deprivation level than others, 3.1 deprivations on average
against 4.2, an advantage that predates treatment and that matching is
designed to neutralize. Once this selection is corrected, sustained
exposure to remittances reduces the number of deprivations by 0.19
among children aged 5 to 14, an effect significant at the ten percent
level; the effect has the same sign but is imprecise among children
aged 0 to 4, and the small number of adolescents followed precludes any
conclusion. The effect runs through the household budget, food
insecurity being the only dimension that clearly declines among
beneficiaries in the first two age groups, and it rises steeply with
the amount received, only transfers in the upper half of the
distribution actually reducing deprivations. These findings outline the
scope and limits of the private flow, which protects what depends on
household spending without reaching infrastructure-related
deprivations: they call for keeping recipient households within the
scope of social protection, for particular attention to preschool-age
children, among whom the protective effect does not materialize, and
for stronger complementarity between private transfers and the public
supply of basic services.

\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Keywords}: child multidimensional poverty, MODA,
migrant remittances, Senegal, EHCVM, propensity score matching.
