\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

In Senegal, children remain deprived across a range of dimensions that
undermine their well-being, with more than half of them affected by
multidimensional poverty in 2018, while close to twenty-four percent (24\%) of
households receive migrant remittances. This paper estimates the causal impact
of remittances on child multidimensional poverty, using the two rounds of the
Harmonized Household Living Standards Survey (EHCVM~I, 2018-2019 and EHCVM~II,
2021-2022). Child poverty is measured through UNICEF's multidimensional (MODA)
approach, based on seven dimensions broken down into 14 indicators, and three
age groups are constructed (0-4, 5-14 and 15-17 years) to capture
differentiated effects along the child's development. Identification combines
propensity score matching (PSM) and difference-in-differences (PSM-DiD
estimator of Heckman et al., 1997-1998), jointly addressing selection on
observables and time-invariant unobservable heterogeneity. The unit of analysis
is the child: the propensity score and the matching are estimated at the
individual level, on children followed across both rounds, using their
household's baseline characteristics together with their own sex and age.
Treated households are those receiving, in 2018, a transfer from a sender
living outside Senegal who had previously been a member of the household --
13.9\% of households and 14.8\% of the children followed. Descriptively, the share of
children deprived in at least four dimensions fell from 63.5\% in 2018-2019 to
57.8\% in 2021-2022, and children in recipient households are less often poor
than others at both dates (46.2\% versus 66.6\% in 2018; 42.5\% versus 60.5\%
in 2021). That level advantage, however, predates treatment: once matching is
applied, the average treatment effect on the treated stands at 0.058 ($p =
0.052$), significant at the 10\% level and unfavourable in sign. Over a three-
year horizon, deprivation among children of recipient households therefore
declines more slowly than among comparable children of non-recipient
households, a result stable across the three matching methods (0.047 to 0.058).
The hypothesis that remittances reduce child poverty is thus rejected. The
effect is not diffuse: it is established only for nutrition (8.1~pp; $p =
0.042$), the only significant dimension out of seven, and among children aged
0 to 4 (7.9~pp; $p = 0.045$). No equality test, however, points to a
differentiated effect -- neither across urban and rural areas ($p = 0.336$),
nor by the child's sex ($p = 0.719$), nor across age groups ($p = 0.495$):
subgroups differ in whether their effect is significant, not in a
statistically distinguishable magnitude. It also
plays out at the margin of poverty: its magnitude follows a bell curve across
the cross-dimensional threshold, peaking at $k=4$ and falling off on either
side. That the effect is established only among the youngest children points to the cost of
the departed adult's absence, though that channel cannot account for the gap on
its own: more than half of non-recipient households also lost an adult between
the two rounds, most often to another region of the country. Receiving remittances is therefore not sufficient, in the
short run, to alter the trajectory of children's multidimensional deprivations
in Senegal: these results call for lower remittance transaction costs, longer-
term support of recipient households so that flows currently devoted two-thirds
to day-to-day consumption translate into durable assets, stronger
complementarity between private transfers and public provision of basic
services, since structural deprivations cannot be lifted by purchasing power
alone, and targeted support for early childhood in households whose adult is
absent through migration.

\bigskip

\noindent \textbf{Keywords}: child multidimensional poverty, MODA, remittances,
Senegal, EHCVM, propensity score matching (PSM), difference-in-differences (DiD).
